Two ordering fixes before anything gets written

Clean before PLEA, not after. PLEA is a chain — each match updates a rating that feeds the next one. If Man Utd and Manchester United are two names in the raw file, PLEA builds two half-finished rating histories and every number downstream is wrong. The cleaning PLEA needs is small (names, dates, no duplicates), so it becomes step 0 and the heavier cleaning stays where you had it.

PLEA must produce a history, not just today's table. "Elos up to date as of today" is enough to rank clubs and nothing else. To train on a 2019 match you need the ratings as they stood the morning of that match. Using today's ratings on a 2019 match hands the model seven years of hindsight and quietly ruins the whole project.

Part one — ratings first
0

Minimal clean, just enough for PLEA

Four fields have to be perfect. Nothing else matters yet.

What PLEA needs

date · home team · away team · home goals · away goals
  • One canonical name per club, applied to both team columns. An unknown name stops the run.
  • No duplicate fixtures, no missing matchdays. Every completed season: 380 matches, 20 clubs, 38 each.
  • Sorted strictly by date, then kickoff time. PLEA is order-dependent — a match out of sequence corrupts everything after it.

The dropping rule — this one bites

Your step 2 says "get rid of matches without certain data we need". Be careful which table you're dropping from.

Never drop a match from PLEA. A missing xG doesn't mean the match wasn't played — and removing it breaks the rating chain for both clubs. Drop rows from the training table in step 2 if you must; the rating history keeps every match that has a score.

PLEA

Premier League Elo Algorithm

Replay every match from August 2010 in date order. Each club carries one number.

v1 spec

expected = 1 / (1 + 10 ** ((opp_elo - own_elo - HOME_ADV) / 400))
actual   = 1.0 win | 0.5 draw | 0.0 loss
margin   = 1.0 if gd == 1
           1.5 if gd == 2
           (11 + gd) / 8 if gd >= 3

new_elo  = own_elo + K * margin * (actual - expected)
Parameterv1 valueWhat it does
K20Reaction speed. Higher = jumpier ratings.
HOME_ADV65Points added to the home side before comparing.
START_ELO1500Everyone's rating in August 2010.
PROMOTED_ELO1400What a newly promoted club walks in on.
CARRY_OVER0.80Between seasons: 1500 + 0.80 * (elo - 1500)

Freeze these five for v1. Don't tune anything until the pipeline runs end to end — then change one at a time and check the score. Every change makes a new version: PLEA v1, PLEA v2. The run log records which version produced which result.

Output: a history table, one row per club per match

date · season · team · opponent · is_home · elo_before · elo_after · opp_elo_before · result

Your ranked table for any season is a query against this, not a separate thing: pick a date, take each club's latest elo_after before it, sort. Changing "current season" is changing the date.

The model only ever reads elo_before. elo_after already knows the result you're predicting.

Test PLEA on its own, before any machine learning

Group every historical match by its Elo gap and check what actually happened. If matches where the gap says 65% produced roughly 65% wins, PLEA works. If they produced 80%, your K is too low; if 55%, too high. You can tune the five parameters on this alone.

Then use PLEA as your first predictor. No model, no features — just "higher adjusted Elo wins". If that beats 45% (always picking the home side), you have a working foundation and a real number to improve on. If it doesn't, something is broken and you've found it before writing any of the hard part.

3

Sanity checks — not a research phase

This step used to be "test every hypothesis". It shouldn't be. The model picks its own features far better than one-at-a-time tests can, so what's left here is smaller, and different in kind.

Why this step exists at all

The model cannot tell you that your data is cheating. Build the rolling-form window the wrong way round, so today's goals leak into today's clues, and it won't complain — it will score 85% and look like a triumph. There is no error message. Everything stays beautiful right up until you predict a real match.

These checks are the only defence, because they're the one place you compare your table against something you already know to be true. You know home teams win about 45% of the time. If the table says otherwise, something is broken — and you've found it in five minutes instead of five weeks.

If you do want to explore a variable, use PLEA as the yardstick

"Do home teams win more?" — yes, obviously, and it tells you nothing. The useful version is: do they win more than their Elo gap says they should? Take PLEA's expected result, subtract it from what happened, and look at what's left. That strips out "better clubs win" and shows you the bit Elo doesn't already know.

Worth doing before you spend an evening building a feature — it's cheap triage on your own time. It is not how you decide what to keep.

Where the real answer comes from

"Which variables matter" is a question you ask the trained model, in step 6 — not the raw data here. It answers it for free, and it answers it properly: every column judged alongside all the others, which no one-at-a-time test can do.

Part three — the call
what

What "the model" actually is

Not a function you write. A file that training produces — and the smallest part of this whole project.

Where it comes from

You hand it the table from step 4: about 6,840 rows, each with roughly 20 numbers and one answer. It reads all of them and gives back a file. That's training — two lines of code, a few seconds to run.

What's inside the file

Fifty flowcharts of yes/no questions. One of them looks roughly like this:

elo_gap > 120 ?
├─ yes → is_home == 1 ?
│        ├─ yes → WIN      (happened 71% of the time)
│        └─ no  → WIN      (58%)
└─ no  → form_points > 1.8 ?
         ├─ yes → WIN      (49%)
         └─ no  → NOT WIN  (34%)

You didn't write elo_gap > 120. The computer found it, by trying every column at every threshold across all 6,840 rows and keeping whichever split separated wins from non-wins best — then doing the same again on each branch. That search is what training means.

How it judges a split

It measures how much cleaner the two resulting groups are — closer to all-wins or all-losses. Say a node holds 1,000 matches, 400 of them wins: a messy 40/60 mix.

split on  elo_gap > 120        split on  kickoff_hour > 15
├─ 300 matches, 70% wins       ├─ 500 matches, 41% wins
└─ 700 matches, 27% wins       └─ 500 matches, 39% wins
   both sides much cleaner        barely moved — useless

It keeps elo_gap and moves on. A column that never produces a clean split never gets used, which is why carrying a useless column costs you almost nothing.

The library (scikit-learn) runs that search, but it isn't a black box — the rule above is the entire algorithm. It's doing a fast brute-force loop, not thinking.

Why fifty and not one

A single flowchart memorises quirks in your particular data. So it builds fifty, each on a random slice of the rows and columns, and they vote.

Where the probability comes from

The vote count, literally. 34 of 50 trees say win, so the answer is 0.68. That is all predict_proba does.

Who does what

You writeThe model works out
Pulling, cleaning, PLEA, the feature table, the 2 × 2 verdict rules, the scoreboard. How much each column matters, and in which combinations.

PLEA is the opposite kind of thing. It's a formula you write and can reason about — you choose K and the home bonus. The model is the reverse: you decide what to show it, it decides what matters. Both live in the project, but only one of them is machine learning.

5

The prediction function

Your step 5, filled in. The short version: you don't write the complicated algorithm — you train it.

The one thing to drop from your plan

"A complicated algorithm with these numbers to construct a percentage" — that is the machine learning model. If you hand-write the weighting rules, you'll be tuning coefficients by feel forever, with no way to tell whether a change helped.

Steps 0–4 exist to produce a clean table. The model learns the weights from it. Your job is the inputs and the scoreboard; the algorithm is discovered, not designed.

What the function actually does

predict(home_team, away_team, date):

  1. look up elo_before for both clubs, as of date
  2. build the feature row from the HOME team's perspective
  3. build the feature row from the AWAY team's perspective
  4. p_home = model.predict_proba(row_home)
     p_away = model.predict_proba(row_away)
  5. read the 2 x 2 below -> one verdict

Same row shape as training, built by the same code. That's what stops next Saturday's prediction from quietly disagreeing with the backtest.

Your four outputs are cleverer than you framed them

You described two ways of doing this and picked the second — ask each side separately, let the numbers not add to 100. That's the right call, and it buys you something you didn't mention: a draw detector, for free.

Two independent questions give four combinations, and the case where both answers are low is exactly what a draw looks like. You get three-outcome behaviour out of a two-outcome model.

Return the numbers alongside the verdict

The verdict is what you show. The two probabilities are what you store. You can always turn numbers into a label later; you can never recover the numbers from a label — and without them you can't tell a 0.51 call from a 0.94 one when you're working out what went wrong.

The scoreboard

  • Baseline to beat — 45%. Always picking the home team. Anything below this is learning noise.
  • PLEA alone. Your second bar, from the test back in part one. The model has to beat the ratings it's built on, or the extra columns aren't earning their place.
  • Log loss / Brier score. Whether the probabilities mean what they say.
  • Verdict accuracy per cell. Track the four outputs separately — "no call" being 40% of your predictions is a real problem that an overall accuracy number hides completely.
  • A run log. Every experiment writes its settings, its PLEA version and its seven scores to a file.

Which variables actually mattered — asked here, not in step 3

rf.feature_importances_ adds up how much each column cleaned up the splits across all fifty trees. One line, free after training, and it judges every column alongside all the others rather than in isolation.

Two things to know. Importance is biased towards columns with many distinct values, so a continuous number will out-rank a 0/1 flag that's doing more real work. And two correlated columns split their credit between them, so both look weaker than either is.

The honest version when it matters: permutation importance — shuffle one column's values and see how far the score falls. Slower, much harder to fool. And the final word on any keep-or-drop decision is still the same: drop it, retrain, check whether the seven-season score got worse.

Expect 2020/21 to look bad

Empty stadiums, home advantage nearly vanished. That round will score worse than its neighbours. It's the season being strange, not the model being broken — note it so you don't chase it.

Weekly, once it's live: pull only the new results → quality gate → extend the PLEA history → rebuild form → retrain → score the upcoming fixtures. Nothing here hard-codes today's date; every function takes it as an argument. That's what makes next August a non-event.

Plain-English glossary

PLEA
Premier League Elo Algorithm. One number per club, up after good results, down after bad, replayed over sixteen seasons of matches.
elo_before / elo_after
A club's rating going into a match and coming out of it. The model only ever sees "before" — "after" already knows the answer.
expected goals (xG)
How good a team's chances were, whether or not they went in. A tap-in is about 0.8, a 30-yard shot about 0.03. Less noisy than goals.
leakage
Letting the model see anything from after kickoff. The most common way a football model looks brilliant in testing and fails for real.
walk-forward testing
Train on the past, test on the next season, step forward, repeat. Seven honest scores rather than one.
calibration
Whether "68%" comes true about 68% of the time. Needed before any number goes on screen.
baseline
The dumbest prediction, used as the bar to clear. Here: "the home team wins", right about 45% of the time.
